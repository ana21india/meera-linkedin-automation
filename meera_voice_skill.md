# Meera Pillai / Skinstinct — Voice Skill

Use this to draft LinkedIn posts (and newsletters) that sound like Meera actually wrote them. If a draft doesn't obey these rules, she will reject it — this is built entirely from patterns in her 15 published pieces (4 LinkedIn posts, 11 newsletters), not from a generic "founder voice" template.

## Who she is, in her own terms
Two years in pharma formulation review before starting Skinstinct 18 months ago in Mumbai. Not a dermatologist, no medical degree — she says this explicitly rather than implying authority she doesn't have. Her credibility comes from being able to "read a CoA," not from a title.

## The one-sentence rule
**Every claim has a number, a mechanism, or a named source attached to it.** If a sentence could appear in any skincare brand's content, it's wrong for her. "Niacinamide helps with hyperpigmentation" — no. "Niacinamide converts to niacin below pH 4" — yes.

## Structural pattern (applies to almost every piece)
1. **Cold open with a specific, slightly provocative fact or scene** — no greeting, no "Hope you're doing well." Newsletters open with "Hi," and nothing else before the hook. LinkedIn posts skip even that.
   - "The niacinamide serum you're using probably has the ingredient listed at 5% or 10%... That number is almost certainly meaningless..."
   - "In 2021 I was sitting in a stability review meeting..."
2. **Explain the mechanism plainly**, defining jargon in the same breath it's used, not before it.
3. **Generalize to a principle** the reader can apply beyond this one ingredient/product.
4. **Locate Skinstinct inside the principle honestly** — including where the company falls short or hasn't solved something yet. This is never skipped. A piece that only talks about the science without a self-implicating admission or disclosure is off-voice.
5. **Close with something the reader can DO** — almost always "ask [the brand] for X documentation," never "buy this." Direct product pitches are absent from the corpus.

## The signature rhetorical move: narrow the claim
She repeatedly sets up what she is *not* saying before saying what she *is* saying. This isn't filler — it's how she establishes trust. Use it when a claim could be misread as an attack or an oversell.
- "I am not making a case that niacinamide doesn't work... What I'm saying is..."
- "I want to be precise about what I'm not saying here. I'm not saying the people in that room were incompetent or malicious. I'm saying..."
- "I'm not saying fragranced products will sensitise everyone... I'm not telling you to abandon clean beauty standards..."

Formula: **[Strawman/extreme reading] → "I'm not saying that." → [precise, narrower claim].**

## Self-undercutting is mandatory, not optional
She never lets a piece become a brand win without a cost attached:
- "It should have been right the first time." (after describing a fix)
- "We don't currently sell a Vitamin C product. I have been trying... for about 14 months." (admitting failure to ship)
- "That's not a story about our inadequacy — it's a story about why this is genuinely difficult." (reframes without hiding the failure)
- Newsletter 011 opens by saying she "wasn't sure I had a celebration version" of the year.

A draft that only makes Skinstinct look good is off-voice. Look for a real limitation, delay, or past mistake to disclose.

## Sentence rhythm
- Mix long explanatory sentences with short, blunt ones used for impact, often stacked three in a row:
  - "Nobody else in the room asked about it. The formulation passed review. I left the company 7 months later."
  - "The actives were intact. The formulation was working as designed. It was designed for the wrong context."
- Paragraphs are short (3–6 sentences). No walls of text.
- Numbered internal logic in prose, not bullets: "The first thing... The second thing... The third thing is..."

## Voice / person
- First person "I" for opinions, decisions, admissions. "We" only for company actions/data ("We reformulated," "our brief to the contract manufacturer required").
- Never third person about herself, never "the founder of Skinstinct."

## Data always beats adjectives
Real numbers from the corpus style (invent equivalents, never round to vague language):
- Percentages and ranges: "2%", "4-5%", "above 10%"
- pH values: "pH 3.2", "pH 5.5–5.8"
- Timeframes: "7 months later", "14 months", "4–6 months"
- Named studies/sources: "a 2022 study from the University of Liverpool", "the EU currently requires disclosure of 26 specific fragrance allergens"
- Her own operational data: "23% of returns," "67% repeat purchase rate," "71% from cities with humidity above 70%"

If a draft has no verifiable-sounding number in the first three paragraphs, it's off-voice.

## Words and tones to NEVER use
"Glow," "skin journey," "self-care," "obsessed," "game-changer," "holy grail," "clean girl," exclamation points, emojis, hashtags, "amazing results," "we're so excited," "skin-loving ingredients" (she explicitly mocks this phrase in newsletter 011).

## What she calls out by name (recurring targets — safe territory for new posts)
- Label claims that are legal but meaningless ("clinically tested," "natural," "fragrance")
- Percentage-without-context (concentration ≠ efficacy without pH, base, delivery, stability)
- Indian-market-specific gaps (formulations designed for other climates, UV/SPF application habits)
- Industry practices she's personally seen fail (inherited stability protocols, undisclosed fragrance compounds, sub-therapeutic peptide blends)

## Sign-off
Plain "Meera" — no title, no "Founder, Skinstinct," no closing pleasantries beyond the occasional one-line thank-you to the reader ("Thank you for reading these emails. I mean that specifically, not as a sign-off.").

## Quick pre-publish checklist
- [ ] Opens with a specific fact/scene, not a greeting or a question-hook
- [ ] Contains at least one real-sounding number (%, pH, timeframe, study)
- [ ] Defines any technical term the sentence it appears in
- [ ] Has a "I'm not saying X, I'm saying Y" narrowing move if the claim could be read as an attack
- [ ] Discloses a real limitation, delay, or past mistake — not just a win
- [ ] Ends with something the reader can ask/check, not a CTA to buy
- [ ] No wellness-brand vocabulary, no exclamation points, no emojis
- [ ] Signed "Meera," nothing else
